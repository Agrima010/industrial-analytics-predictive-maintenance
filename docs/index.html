1 · The story starts here

A morning on the metro

Imagine you run the metro in Porto, Portugal. Every morning a timetable promises thousands of passengers that a train will arrive every few minutes. To keep that promise, enough trains must leave the depot healthy, safe and ready.

Around midday, a driver sees a low-pressure warning. Somewhere on the train, compressed air is escaping. The train can still move for now, but without enough air the brakes, doors and suspension cannot work properly. The train is taken out of service. Passengers get off at the next station, the following trips are cancelled, and a maintenance crew that had planned a quiet afternoon now has an emergency.

Now imagine the same day with one change: two days earlier, a monitoring system had noticed that the train’s air compressor was working harder than usual. The maintenance planner booked a one-hour inspection for that night in the depot. A loose fitting was tightened. The next morning, nobody noticed anything.

The difference between those two days is what this project is about. The first is reactive maintenance: repair after something breaks. The second is predictive maintenance: use data to see the problem coming and fix it at a convenient time.

The scene is illustrative. The operational facts behind it come from Veloso et al. (2022): the air unit has no backup, its failure removes a train from service, and more than 170 trips were cancelled for this kind of reason in 2017.

2 · Context

The machine behind the doors

A metro train runs on electricity, but many of its systems run on compressed air. The brakes use it, the doors open and close with it, and the secondary suspension uses air springs to keep the floor at platform height whether the car carries ten passengers or two hundred.

That air comes from the Air Production Unit (APU), installed on the roof of each Metro do Porto vehicle. An electric motor drives a compressor, the air is cleaned and dried, and then stored in reservoirs until the train’s systems need it. The APU is in demand all day. Because there is no backup unit, an APU failure means the train must be removed from service immediately (Veloso et al., 2022).

3 · How it normally works

The compressor’s heartbeat

The compressor does not run all the time. It works in a rhythm, like a heartbeat:

  1. The train uses air for braking and doors, so the pressure in the reservoirs slowly falls.
  2. When it drops below about 8.2 bar, a signal (MPG) starts the compressor under load.
  3. The compressor refills the tanks quickly, up to about 10 bar.
  4. It then switches to idle (offloaded) or stops, and the cycle begins again.

Davari et al. (2021) call these two phases the run time (filling) and the idle time (consuming). On a pressure chart they make a saw-tooth pattern. The shape of that saw-tooth tells an expert a lot about the health of the system.

The real data confirms the rhythm. Across all seven months, the motor current shows the compressor is off more than half of the time and genuinely working under load only about one-eighth of the time.

4 · What goes wrong

When the air leaks

A component rarely stops without warning. An air leak usually starts small: a worn seal, a loose pipe, a valve that does not close fully. The train still runs, but the compressor quietly works harder to make up for the lost air. Eventually it cannot keep up, pressure drops below safe levels, and the train is withdrawn.

In the period covered by our dataset, the operator’s maintenance reports record four air-leak failures, all rated “high stress”. One simple measure, the duty cycle (the share of running time that the compressor spends under load), already shows them clearly.

5 · Business understanding

The business problem

A metro operator runs two operations that depend on each other. Train operations make sure scheduled trips happen. Maintenance operations inspect and repair trains so they are available for those trips. When a train breaks down in service, the two collide: a trip is lost and the workshop has to drop its plan.

An unexpected failure costs the operator in several ways at once: the interrupted journey, cancelled trips that follow, a train out of the fleet, overtime or re-planning in the workshop, and lost trust from passengers (Veloso et al., 2022). The air unit also tends to fail in ways that fixed alarm thresholds do not catch early, because they only fire once pressure is already too low.

There are three broad ways to maintain a machine like this:

Reactive / corrective

Fix it when it breaks

Wait for a failure, then repair.

  • No wasted parts or inspections
  • Every failure is a surprise in service
  • Cancelled trips, emergency work
Preventive

Fix it on a calendar

Inspect or replace parts at fixed intervals.

  • Fewer surprises
  • Healthy parts get replaced too early
  • Still misses failures between visits
Predictive · our focus

Fix it when the data says so

Watch the machine’s condition and act when it starts to drift.

  • Repairs planned into depot time
  • Parts used for their full life
  • Depends on warnings arriving early and being trustworthy

Predictive maintenance only creates value if the warning arrives early enough to act on. Workshops schedule jobs in blocks of hours or days, not seconds (Navinchandran et al., 2019), so a warning one minute before failure is useless. A warning that keeps firing for no reason is also useless, because planners stop trusting it. The business value sits in that balance.

6 · Stakeholders

Who is affected

Passengers

Feel every failure as a cancelled trip or a forced change of train. They are the reason availability matters.

Train operations

Dispatchers and drivers who need enough healthy trains to run the timetable, and who must react when one is withdrawn.

Maintenance team

Planners and technicians who would receive the alerts. They want early, specific warnings and few false alarms.

Fleet & asset management

Decide on spare parts, depot capacity and maintenance strategy. They care about cost, availability and reliability figures like MTBF and MTTR.

7 · The question

Our research question

Research question

Can an anomaly-detection model warn early enough, before compressor degradation becomes operationally critical, for maintenance to be planned instead of done reactively?

The key idea is lead time: the time between the first persistent alarm and the start of the failure. Metro do Porto stated that a failure must be detected at least two hours before the train becomes non-operational, so it can be safely taken off the line (Veloso et al., 2022; Davari et al., 2021). We use two hours as our minimum and will report how much more our models achieve.

8 · Data understanding

The evidence we have

We use MetroPT-3, a public real-world dataset published by INESC TEC and the University of Porto on the UCI Machine Learning Repository. It was recorded on the APU of one operating Metro do Porto train. The logger ran at 1 Hz; the public CSV we work with holds one reading roughly every 10 seconds.

The data has no labels: nothing in the file says “this is a failure”. The only ground truth is the company’s failure reports in Section 4. That is why we use unsupervised anomaly detection: the model learns what normal looks like and flags what does not fit.

10 · Solution approach

How we will answer it

We follow Gröger’s (2022) four levels of industrial analytics, each building on the last, and the evaluation protocol proposed with the dataset (Veloso et al., 2022).

Descriptive

What happened?

Signal distributions, operating states, duty cycles, and behaviour around the failure windows.

Diagnostic

Why did it happen?

Which signals change before and during leaks, explained with the APU’s physics: pressure-drop rates, run time, cycle frequency.

Predictive

What will happen?

Unsupervised anomaly detection: a rolling z-score baseline, Isolation Forest, One-Class SVM, and an autoencoder as in Davari et al. (2021).

Prescriptive

What should we do?

Turn the anomaly score into a maintenance decision: alert threshold, persistence rule, and the cost of false alarms vs. missed failures.

Rules we will not break. Models learn only from periods assumed healthy, never from failure data. We split by time, never at random, because this is a time series. The simple statistical baseline is always reported next to the complex models. With only four failures we report each event separately and do not over-claim.

Benchmark. On a longer list of 21 expert-reported events, Davari et al. (2021) found that a sparse autoencoder on analog signals caught air-leak failures, with 7 true and 3 false positives, while the digital-signal version reached 62 % F1. Their alarm rule (a smoothed score that must stay low for a sequence of cycles) is a useful template for our persistence rule.

Business and operational targets: at least 2 hours of advance warning so a train can be rerouted to the depot instead of blocking the line; fewer trips cancelled by sudden APU failures (against the 170+ cancellations reported for 2017, not all of which were caused by compressors); and a higher share of planned interventions compared with emergency repairs.

12 · Scope

Scope, limits and next steps

In scope

  • Early detection of air-leak failures on one APU, from sensor data alone
  • Lead time, false alarms and event recall, measured against the four reported failures
  • A simple alert rule a planner could act on, with its cost trade-offs

Known limits

  • Only four failures, all the same type: low statistical power
  • One train, one APU: results may not hold for the whole fleet
  • Failure windows are coarse report times; one maintenance date is inconsistent
  • About 18 % of the time has no data, and our CSV is at 10 s, not 1 Hz
